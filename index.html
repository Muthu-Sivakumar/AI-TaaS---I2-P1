About

Four decades in technology, then the work that mattered more

Raj Asava came to the United States from India and built a career across senior and executive roles at large technology companies, including two stints as a startup president. He retired in 2010 as Chief Strategy Officer of Perot Systems.

A year earlier, his wife Aradhana had left her own twenty-year career in global IT to focus on philanthropy. In 2015, a conversation with the mayor of Plano introduced them both to something they had never seen in their own affluent suburb: children who went without full meals between Friday afternoon and Monday morning.

They decided to give three years to the problem. In September 2017 they launched HungerMitao, wrote the first $100,000 check themselves, and started asking the Indian American community to give where it lives. The movement has since spread to metro regions across the country and enabled tens of millions of meals through Feeding America member food banks.

Raj and Aradhana remain full-time volunteers. Alongside hunger, they support children's advocacy, literacy, and mentoring the next generation.

Speaking

Talks on second acts, civic giving, and what a community can do when it decides to

Each talk works as a 20-minute keynote, a 45-minute session with questions, or a fireside conversation. Raj adjusts the examples to fit the audience in front of him.

The second act

Forty years of corporate work ends with a retirement party, and then what? Raj talks about leaving the Chief Strategy Officer title behind and finding that the most demanding work of his life was still in front of him. He is honest about how uncertain that transition felt, and about what it takes to start something at an age when most people are winding down.

Best for: corporate leadership audiences, executive networks, retirement and transition programs

Audience takeaways

  • How to find the problem worth your second act
  • Why starting small beat starting big
  • Giving yourself a fixed trial period

Hunger in plain sight

Raj had lived in American suburbs for decades and had seen only affluence. Then he learned that children a few miles away had no full meal between Friday lunch and Monday breakfast. This talk is about the gap between what we think we see in our own neighborhoods and what is actually there, and what changes once people decide to look.

Best for: nonprofit galas, food bank events, civic organizations, faith communities

Audience takeaways

  • The real shape of food insecurity in the US
  • Why proximity changes willingness to give
  • Where a single volunteer actually helps

Mobilizing a community

HungerMitao was built on a simple idea: a community already giving generously, but in scattered directions, becomes powerful when it points at one cause together. Raj walks through how the model was built, how it spread city by city, and how other communities adapted it for themselves during the pandemic.

Best for: diaspora and cultural organizations, philanthropy networks, universities

Audience takeaways

  • Focusing fragmented giving on one cause
  • Movement versus organization, and why it matters
  • Handing the model to another community
